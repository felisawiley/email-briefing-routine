# Motivation

Nick Maggiulli’s latest essay challenges a quiet personal-finance assumption: that a dollar of spending power buys the same amount of life at every age. It doesn’t. Drawing on “Janet’s Law” — subjective time scales with how much life you’ve already lived — and the reminiscence bump (our densest memories cluster around ages 10–30), he argues a year in your twenties occupies roughly five times more memorable life than a year in your sixties. Money spent on the right experiences earlier can pay a higher lived dividend.

Compounding is the counterweight. At about a 4% real return, $100 invested at 25 can become roughly $500 (inflation-adjusted) by 65 — almost exactly offsetting that experiential premium. Believe you’ll earn more, and saving wins; believe less, and present spending looks more rational than the spreadsheet admits. The cruel part is timing: the years when money buys the most lived experience are usually the years you have the least of it. The quieter failure mode is the opposite — getting excellent at accumulating money you never convert back into a life.

Robert Glazer’s *Friday Forward* (#547) offered a smaller companion lesson from a UPS loading dock: when policy says no, rapport still sometimes opens a side door. Escalation makes people defend the rule; treating the person as a person gives them a reason to use the discretion the rule pretends they don’t have.

# Motivation

Nick Maggiulli’s latest essay reframes a quiet assumption in personal finance: that a dollar of spending power buys the same amount of life at every age. It doesn’t.

Drawing on “Janet’s Law” — the idea that subjective time is proportional to how much life you’ve already lived — Maggiulli argues that a year in your twenties occupies far more of your memorable life than a year in your sixties. Adjust for the reminiscence bump (our densest memories cluster around ages 10–30), and the gap looks something like five-to-one. If experience is valuable partly because we relive it, then money spent on the right things earlier pays a higher experiential dividend.

That does **not** become a license to torch the future. Compounding is the counterweight. At roughly a 4% real return, $100 invested at 25 can become about $500 (inflation-adjusted) by 65 — almost exactly offsetting the experiential premium of spending it young. Believe you’ll earn more than that, and saving wins. Believe you’ll earn less, or don’t believe in investing at all, and present spending looks more rational than the spreadsheet admits.

The cruel part is timing: the years when money buys the most lived experience are also the years when you usually have the least of it. The quieter failure mode is the opposite — becoming excellent at accumulating money you never convert back into a life. Maggiulli’s counsel lands in the middle: don’t feel guilty about buying the trip, the concert, the season of your life that won’t be available later in the same body. Money can purchase a lot. It cannot repurchase youth’s novelty, health, or unfinished firsts.

Robert Glazer’s *Friday Forward* (#547) offered a smaller, complementary lesson from a UPS loading dock: when policy says no, rapport still sometimes opens a side door. Escalation makes people defend the rule. Treating the person as a person gives them a reason to use the discretion the rule pretends they don’t have.

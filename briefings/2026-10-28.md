# Motivation

Nick Maggiulli's question is the one younger savers keep asking: if the trust fund is "going bankrupt," should you plan as if Social Security will be zero?

No. The retirement trust fund is projected to run dry in 2032. After that, payroll taxes still cover most of the checks — about 78 percent of scheduled benefits in the first year, falling toward 62 percent by 2100 if Congress never acts. That is a cut, not a disappearance. The average benefit today is about $2,000 a month; 78 percent of that is $1,560. Maggiulli plans at 70 percent of the SSA estimate, not zero and not the full amount.

The formula is progressive: your 35 highest wage-indexed years, then bend points that replace more of low earnings than high ones. Claiming at 62, 67, or 70 moves the number a lot. The practical move is to look up your SSA estimate and multiply by 0.7. If you need $8,000 a month and Social Security is $2,100, the gap is $5,900 a month — about $1.8 million at a 4 percent withdrawal.

Congress can raise taxes, cut benefits, or both. Public support leans toward higher taxes rather than smaller checks. Plan for most of the benefit. Do not build the whole retirement on it. Assuming the check will not arrive is the bigger planning error than counting on the full amount.

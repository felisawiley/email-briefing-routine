Nobody sits a first-time founder down and says the paperwork already made a board. Incorporating puts you and your co-founder in the seats whether you noticed. A small angel check can come with a request for a vote you did not need to give away. A SAFE feels like freedom because the investor has almost no formal claim — until the priced round, when preferred rights arrive all at once. Walsh’s room (J.P. Morgan, Girl Math Capital, a governance lawyer) put a number on it: founders still hold voting control in 71% of companies after Series A, down from 88% at seed. The typical board goes from three seats to five. The independent director is the one you can still choose if you seat them before the term sheet names someone else.

That is Glazer’s alarm and Maggiulli’s tax overlay pointed at a cap table. You can manufacture a causal story so the meeting can end, or manufacture losses so the basis never gets touched, or manufacture a “we’ll figure out the board later” so the raise can close. All three feel like progress. Walsh’s test is the one to take into Glazer’s agent training this morning: who is legally on your board, and can you name one decision they ratified this quarter? If you cannot answer that cleanly, you are exposed. The decisions that feel too small to matter in month one are the ones that decide how much control you have by Series A.

# Motivation

Nick Maggiulli’s *The Problem with Optionality* pushes back on the internet’s favorite life strategy: keep every door open, grind alone, don’t commit until you’re “ready.” That logic works for diversified portfolios. Applied to careers, partners, and identity, it often produces maximizers who are less satisfied, less optimistic, and more depressed than people who choose and move on.

The hidden invoice shows up late. Every year spent preserving options is a year not spent on the thing you eventually pick — less cumulative time with a partner, fewer overlapping years with grandchildren, a career still staged as a placeholder. Options are essential when you truly don’t know what you like; once you do, their marginal value falls and their opportunity cost rises.

Commitment also rewires preference. Classic painting experiments (including with amnesic participants who couldn’t remember which canvas they “chose”) show that once you pick, you like your pick more and the rejected option less — not as self-justifying spin, but as a real preference shift. Maggiulli’s close: commit to yourself, yes — but the best self-commitment is choosing the few things that make a life worth living, not optimizing for infinite exits.

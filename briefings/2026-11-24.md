# Motivation

The scare line is that Social Security goes to zero when the trust fund empties. Maggiulli’s quieter math: the retirement trust is projected to run dry around 2032, but payroll taxes keep coming in. Benefits would fall to about 78 percent of what was scheduled that first year — a cut, not a disappearance. Plan on something like 70 percent and you are closer to the actual risk than the headline.

The average check is about $2,000 a month. Seventy-eight percent of that is still a floor. The bigger mistake, he argues, is writing the program off entirely and saving as if the floor were zero. Congress is more likely to raise taxes than to let the check vanish; voters say they want benefits kept even if someone pays more.

A usable move: look up your estimated benefit, multiply by 0.7, and treat only the rest as something you have to fund. If you need $8,000 a month and Social Security might cover $2,100, the gap is what the savings pile is for. Do not build a plan on the full scheduled check. Do not build one on nothing either.

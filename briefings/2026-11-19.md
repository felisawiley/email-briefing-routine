# Motivation

Offloading thinking is older than chatbots. We already hand arithmetic to a calculator, a diagnosis to a doctor, and a count to our fingers. Cognitive psychologist Trent Cash’s point, in a *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* review Leigh Christopher walked through overnight, is that the scare story is usually aimed at the wrong layer. Basic abilities like attention and memory are not what AI is stealing. Skills are. Skills are learned by practice, and they rot when you stop doing the work.

That is why the high-school math study matters. Students with a general-purpose chatbot did better while the bot was there and worse once it was gone. Students with a tutor that refused to hand over the answer did not pay that bill. The tool that completes the task for you is the one that removes the practice.

The rule of thumb is blunt: do not consistently offload a skill you still want. A writer who never writes, a radiologist who never reads the image, an analyst who never touches the data will be fine until the tool is down or wrong. Stay in the loop — review the code, ask why the itinerary looks like that, make the model brainstorm instead of finish the assignment. The skills you are willing to lose can go. The ones that are the job cannot.

# Motivation

Bravery is not saying the thing your tribe will clap for. That is cheap. Real bravery, as Jill Schulman and psychologist Cynthia Pury define it, is acting toward a worthwhile goal while fear or risk is still in the room. Robert Glazer calls the shortage of that a bravery recession: people keep their more interesting opinions quiet until a promotion, a friendship, or standing in the group is no longer on the line.

We are wired for it. Loss hurts about twice as much as an equal gain feels good, and a lot of current advice tells people to step away from discomfort instead of through it. Avoidance is not free. A Dutch study that followed about 2,500 adults for four years found that habitually dodging uncomfortable thoughts and feelings made depression more likely to start or stick.

The way through is smaller than it sounds. Schulman shook her way down a forty-foot Navy SEAL confidence-course zipline and found the fear lived mostly in the anticipation. Glazer had the same click on a high ropes course: one foot, then the next, with the harness holding. Surround yourself with people who have done the hard thing and will not let you tap out. Then do the hard thing anyway, knowing a truly brave call usually costs something.

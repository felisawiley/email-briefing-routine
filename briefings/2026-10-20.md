# Motivation

Kevin Johnson’s parking-garage story starts with a used Mercedes and a little auction sticker. A colleague recognized the Manheim decal immediately — he had been buying cars there “forever.” Johnson had known him forever too. The subject had never come up.

IYKYK is usually a wink. The other side is: if you don’t know, that’s your problem. Johnson spent years buying new cars at dealerships because that is what responsible adults do. Then a high-school friend who sold cars invited him to the auction, explained the economics, and he went home with a better car and leftover credit-union cash to kill a high-interest card. Later he learned several colleagues already used the same path. He does not think they were plotting against him. Advantages become invisible to the people who hold them. And sometimes people keep the map because sharing it would raise the price.

That is the leadership test. Not every private knowledge is an injustice, but a lot of careers run on unwritten rules: which mentor to pick, which hallway the decision actually happens in, which auction exists at all. Sharing an intro or an unwritten rule can cost you a scarce slot. It is also what the people who helped you did. Johnson’s friend could have marked the car up. He took him along instead.

If you know, you know. Tell someone else.

# Motivation

Income multiples make a tidy chart and a bad scorecard. Fidelity-style targets — one times salary by 30, ten times by 67 — assume a stable paycheck. A promotion, a commission year, or a layoff would rewrite the target overnight. Maggiulli’s point is blunter: more than 90 percent of U.S. households miss those marks anyway, and a third of households in every age band have no retirement account at all.

The number that matters is spending, not income. Two households earning the same salary can have very different retirements if one lives on half of it and the other does not. A higher savings rate shrinks what you need later and gets you there sooner. Social Security also replaces more of a lower earner’s paycheck, so one multiple for everyone is the wrong ruler.

A cleaner check: pick annual retirement spending in today’s dollars, subtract Social Security or a pension, multiply the gap by 25, then discount that future pile back at 5 percent real for the years you have left. Compare what you have now to that current need. The gap should be widest when you start working and should close as you save. If you have already hit the current number, you can coast. If you are behind, save more or spend less later — and do not panic-compare yourself to someone else’s 10x.
